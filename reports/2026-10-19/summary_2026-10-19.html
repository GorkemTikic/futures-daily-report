🗿 The day, explained simply

Bitcoin fell by 0.2% and Ethereum fell by 0.1% on Monday, October 19. It was a quiet day—not a crash, just a small decline. What mattered more was that really big companies kept buying lots of Bitcoin (Strive spent $335 million, and JPMorgan brought its dollar token to the Ethereum network). Computer chip companies in Asia rose sharply (Samsung and SK Hynix went up 5-6%) because everyone wants chips for artificial intelligence. New tools also launched: Mastercard and JPMorgan made fraud checkers for crypto payments, and Mesh connected to Visa so you can spend crypto at regular stores. Overall: a calm day with good long-term news about adoption, even though prices fell a tiny bit.

07 · Glossary

Every term used today, in plain words

Adoption — When more people or companies start using or accepting cryptocurrency.
Annualized — Expressed as a yearly rate. A funding rate of 0.5% per 8-hour period annualized becomes much higher when compounded over 365 days.
Bitcoin — The original and largest cryptocurrency, created in 2009. Often used as the primary reference asset for crypto markets.
Blockchain — The underlying technology behind cryptocurrencies—a decentralized ledger that records transactions across many computers.
Breadth — The percentage of cryptocurrency markets moving up vs. down on a given day. High breadth (e.g., 59% up) suggests broad participation in gains.
Deleveraging — When traders reduce borrowed funds or close leveraged positions, often forcing price moves downward when many do so at once.
ETF (Exchange-Traded Fund) — A fund that holds cryptocurrency or commodities and trades on a stock exchange like a regular stock. Allows people to own crypto exposure without managing private keys.
Ethereum — The second-largest cryptocurrency by market value, known for supporting smart contracts and decentralized applications.
Funding rate — A periodic payment (typically every 8 hours) between long and short traders on futures contracts. Positive rates mean long traders pay short traders, signaling optimism; negative rates go the other way.
Institutional — Large organizations like banks, investment firms, or corporations (as opposed to individual retail traders).
Leverage / leveraged — Borrowing money to make a larger bet. Amplifies both gains and losses.
Liquidation — When a leveraged position is forcibly closed due to losses exceeding the trader's initial margin.
Long / long position — A bet that an asset's price will rise. A long trader profits if the price goes up.
Long/short ratio — The proportion of traders betting that prices will rise (longs) versus those betting prices will fall (shorts). A ratio above 1 means more longs than shorts.
Mark price — The current price used by a futures exchange to calculate profit and loss on derivatives positions. It may differ slightly from the spot price.
Open interest — The total notional value of all futures positions open at a given time. Rising open interest suggests traders are adding new positions; falling open interest means positions are closing.
Perpetual futures / perps — Derivative contracts that never expire, allowing traders to bet on price movements with leverage. Settlements happen via the funding rate.
Short / short position — A bet that an asset's price will fall. A short trader profits if the price goes down.
Stablecoin — A cryptocurrency designed to maintain a stable value, usually pegged to the US dollar (e.g., USDC, USDT, USAT).
Taker buy/sell ratio — The fraction of market orders (takers) that are buys versus sells. A ratio above 1 means more aggressive buying than selling.
Token — A digital asset built on a blockchain. Some tokens represent cryptocurrencies; others represent access to platforms or shared ownership.
Top trader / top position ratio — The long/short balance among the largest traders on an exchange. Signals what big money is betting on.
Treasury (crypto treasury) — Holdings of cryptocurrency kept on a corporation's balance sheet as an asset, similar to cash reserves.
Venue — A cryptocurrency exchange or trading platform (e.g., Binance, Bybit, OKX).
