🗿 The day, explained simply

Bitcoin went up about 1% and Ethereum went up about half a percent on October 9. The biggest story was a theft: hackers stole somewhere between $72 million and $90 million in crypto from people who bought hardware wallets (special devices like USB drives for storing cryptocurrency) from a company in Southeast Asia called CryptoBilis. Ledger, which makes these wallets, is investigating but doesn't yet know if the devices were fake, broken into before shipping, or tampered with after delivery. That same day, another big story broke: Tether (the company that makes a popular crypto stablecoin called USDT) froze about $1.45 million in cryptocurrency that was being held by THORChain, a decentralized trading platform, for about 27 minutes. This showed that Tether can control people's money even when it's supposed to be in decentralized systems. Meanwhile, investors were pulling money out of Bitcoin and Ethereum ETFs (investment funds that track these coins)—Bitcoin ETFs lost $244 million and Ethereum ETFs lost $72.5 million in a single day. It was a worrying day for security and centralization concerns, even though the prices of Bitcoin and Ethereum themselves stayed relatively stable.

Ledger investigates $72–90 million hardware wallet theft tied to Southeast Asian reseller
Blockchain investigators estimate $72–90 million in suspected crypto thefts from Ledger hardware wallet buyers who purchased devices from reseller CryptoBilis in Southeast Asia. Ledger disclosed the incident on October 9 and is investigating whether devices were counterfeit, physically tampered with, or compromised through another method. Tether freezes ~$1.45 million in THORChain TRON vaults for approximately 27 minutes
Tether blocklisted four of THORChain's six TRON vaults on October 9, temporarily halting the platform's TRON trading and causing approximately $363,000 in payouts to queue. The incident illustrates that while THORChain distributes transaction-signing authority across validator nodes, issuer Tether retains the ability to freeze stablecoins unilaterally, raising concerns about centralized control over supposedly decentralized infrastructure.

07 · Glossary

Every term used today, in plain words

Blocklisted — Added to a blacklist so that a cryptocurrency issuer (like Tether) can prevent transfers from those addresses, effectively freezing the funds held there.
Designated Contract Market (DCM) — A regulated US futures exchange licensed by the CFTC to offer futures contracts and event contracts to the public.
ETF (Exchange-traded fund) — An investment fund traded on a stock exchange that tracks an asset or index (in this case, Bitcoin or Ethereum).
Funding rate — A periodic payment between traders holding long and short positions in perpetual futures contracts; positive rates mean longs pay shorts, negative rates mean shorts pay longs. Annualized funding expresses this as a yearly percentage.
Futures Commission Merchant (FCM) — A US firm licensed by the CFTC to act as a broker, handling customer funds and risk management for derivatives trading.
Hardware wallet — A physical device (like a USB drive) used to securely store and manage cryptocurrency private keys offline, reducing hacking risk compared to online storage.
Long-to-short ratio — The proportion of traders holding bullish (long) positions versus bearish (short) positions in a market. Above 1.0 means more longs; below 1.0 means more shorts.
Mark price — The price used by futures exchanges to calculate unrealized gains and losses and to determine when to liquidate underfunded positions. Usually follows the underlying asset's spot price closely.
Market breadth — The percentage of assets in a market that went up (or down) on a given day, showing whether gains/losses were broad-based or concentrated in a few assets.
Net inflow / Net outflow — The total amount of money flowing into (inflow) or out of (outflow) an investment product like an ETF on a given day.
Open interest — The total value of all outstanding futures contracts in a market, measured in either coins or USD. An increase signals growing leverage and conviction; a decrease signals positions closing.
Reseller — A company authorized by a manufacturer to sell its products to end customers, typically for distribution in a specific geographic region.
Spot ETF — An ETF that holds the actual underlying asset (Bitcoin or Ethereum coins) directly rather than futures contracts, allowing investors to own the crypto without managing wallets.
Stablecoin — A cryptocurrency designed to maintain a stable price, usually pegged to a real-world asset like the US dollar (e.g., USDT or USDC).
Swap — In regulatory terms, a derivatives contract between two parties where payments are exchanged based on price movements of an underlying asset. Under CFTC rules, swaps require specific licenses to trade.
Taker buy/sell ratio — A ratio comparing the volume of aggressive buy orders (takers) to aggressive sell orders. Above 1.0 means more buying pressure; below 1.0 means more selling pressure.
