🗿 The day, explained simply

Bitcoin had a scary, wild day on Saturday. Early in the day it dropped to $57,000—the lowest it's been in two years. People panicked and started selling, which forced traders who had borrowed money to buy Bitcoin to sell their positions quickly. This wiped out $1.8 billion in bets. But later in the day Bitcoin bounced back up and closed around $60,200, up 2% from where it started. Ethereum also climbed 3.9%. The bad news: Chainflip, a protocol that moves money between blockchains, got hacked for $4.86 million when someone stole the system's signing key. The whole market was extremely nervous (fear level stayed at 12, the most extreme), so big investors were staying away.

07 · Glossary

Every term used today, in plain words

Collateral — Assets locked up as security for a loan or leveraged position. If the value of your collateral falls below a certain level, your position can be automatically closed (liquidated).
ETF (Exchange-Traded Fund) — A financial product that tracks the price of an asset (like Bitcoin or Ethereum) and trades on a stock exchange. US spot ETFs hold actual Bitcoin or Ethereum, not futures contracts.
ETF inflow / outflow — When investors buy into an ETF (inflow), new Bitcoin or Ethereum is added to the fund. When investors sell out (outflow), Bitcoin or Ethereum is removed. Net outflows mean more people are selling than buying.
Fear & Greed Index — A sentiment gauge ranging from 0 (Extreme Fear) to 100 (Extreme Greed). Extreme Fear means the market is very scared; Extreme Greed means people are overconfident.
Funding rate — A periodic payment between traders in perpetual futures contracts. When funding is positive, bullish traders (longs) pay bearish traders (shorts); when negative, shorts pay longs. High funding rates suggest traders are overconfident in one direction.
Liquidation — When a trader's leveraged position is forcibly closed because their collateral value fell below the minimum. The exchange automatically sells their position, often at a loss, to cover the debt.
Long / short — A long position is a bet that an asset's price will rise. A short position is a bet that it will fall. The long/short ratio shows how many traders are betting on price increases versus decreases.
Long/short ratio — The proportion of traders holding long positions versus short positions. A ratio above 1 means more longs (bullish). A ratio below 1 means more shorts (bearish).
Mark price — The reference price used by exchanges to calculate whether a leveraged position should be liquidated. It's usually a weighted average of prices across multiple venues to prevent manipulation.
Market breadth — The percentage of coins in the market that are rising versus falling. High breadth (more coins up) suggests broad strength; low breadth suggests weakness is concentrated in a few assets.
Open interest — The total number of open (unfilled) leveraged positions on a perpetual futures contract. Rising open interest suggests traders are adding more leverage; falling open interest suggests positions are closing.
Taker buy / sell ratio — The ratio of market buy orders to sell orders. Above 1 means more aggressive buying pressure; below 1 means more aggressive selling pressure. Signals what type of trader is driving price moves.
Top-position ratio — The long/short ratio among the largest traders (highest-volume accounts). Useful because big traders' positions often move price more than retail traders.
