Bitcoin and Ethereum both fell 3–4.5% on September 15 as the U.S. Senate failed to advance crypto-market regulation (the Clarity Act) and traders prepared for an expected Federal Reserve interest-rate decision.

🦴 Caveman mode
🗿 The day, explained simply

Bitcoin and Ethereum are types of digital money that people buy and sell. Today both of them went down in price, which means people were selling them. This happened because of two big things: First, the U.S. government was trying to make new rules about cryptocurrency, but the lawmakers in the Senate voted against it and it failed — so no new rules happened today. Second, people think the U.S. Federal Reserve (the bank that runs America's money) is going to announce tomorrow that it will make it more expensive to borrow money, which worries people. When people worry, they sell their Bitcoin and Ethereum. So it was a worrying day, but not a panic — the price went down but prices did not crash.

07 · Glossary

Every term used today, in plain words

Annualized funding rate — The rate (expressed as a yearly percentage) that traders who hold long positions pay to traders who hold short positions in perpetual futures contracts. If the rate is positive, longs pay shorts. A high funding rate often signals strong bullish sentiment (too many longs), while a negative rate signals bearish sentiment (too many shorts).
Basis — The calendar day or date used to calculate a price movement. 'utc-day' means the day is calculated from 00:00 to 23:59 UTC. 'rolling-24h' means the data covers the most recent 24 hours from the time of measurement.
Cloture vote — A procedural Senate vote to end debate and force a final vote on a bill. It requires 60 votes to pass. If cloture fails, the bill cannot move forward to a final vote.
Funding rate — In perpetual futures markets, an interest rate paid between traders to keep the perpetual price in line with the spot price. See 'annualized funding rate' for the yearly equivalent.
Long/short ratio — The ratio of long positions (bets that price will rise) to short positions (bets that price will fall) among traders in a market. A ratio above 1.0 means more longs than shorts, typically signaling bullish sentiment; below 1.0 means more shorts.
Mark price — The reference price used in perpetual futures markets, calculated as a weighted average of spot prices across exchanges. Funding payments are based on mark price to prevent manipulation.
MEV (maximal extractable value) — Profit that a miner or validator can earn by reordering, inserting, or withholding transactions in a block. MEV bots scan mempool transactions and can front-run (trade ahead of) pending orders to profit from price movements.
Open interest — The total number of outstanding (unsettled) futures or perpetual contracts in a market. Rising open interest suggests increased leverage and positioning; falling open interest suggests traders are closing positions or taking risk off.
Open-interest change — The percentage increase or decrease in total open interest over a given period (in this report, the calendar day). Positive change means more positions were opened than closed; negative means more closed than opened.
Perpetual futures contract — A derivative contract that allows traders to bet on price movements without an expiration date, unlike traditional futures. Perpetuals can be held indefinitely and include funding-rate payments to keep the price aligned with spot.
Spot price — The current market price of an asset for immediate delivery (as opposed to futures, which are contracts for delivery in the future).
Tokenized assets — Traditional financial securities (stocks, bonds, commodities) or real-world assets converted into blockchain-based tokens. Tokenized assets can be traded 24/7 on crypto platforms and may offer higher yield or lower fees than traditional settlement.
Volume — The total dollar value (or number of contracts) traded in a market over a given period. Higher volume typically indicates more confidence in price discovery and liquidity.
