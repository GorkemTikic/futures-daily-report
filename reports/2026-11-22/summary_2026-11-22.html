🗿 The day, explained simply

Bitcoin and Ethereum both went up about 1.7 to 2.3% today—a good day for them. Some smaller coins crashed hard: one called EVAA dropped 64%, while another called BULLA jumped 60%. The bigger problem was that several companies that run bridges (digital tunnels people use to move crypto between different blockchains) got hacked. A protocol called Chainflip lost $12 million, Fusionist lost $2 million or more, and other bridge companies had problems too. Some exchanges in South Korea even had to stop letting people move their crypto because of these hacks. Even though Bitcoin and Ethereum went up and traders got a little less scared, it was still a worrying day because of all the security problems.

07 · Glossary

Every term used today, in plain words

Basis — The difference between a futures price and the spot price. In crypto, basis shows how much traders are willing to pay for leveraged exposure above the cash market price.
Breadth — The percentage of coins or tokens that rose on the day versus those that fell. 66.7% breadth means two-thirds of Binance perpetuals went up, showing broad-based market strength.
Bridge — A digital tunnel that lets traders move crypto from one blockchain to another. For example, a bridge lets you move Bitcoin-based tokens to Ethereum or other chains. Bridges rely on code to lock and unlock funds, and exploits happen when hackers find bugs in that code.
Fear & Greed Index — A score from 0 (Extreme Fear) to 100 (Extreme Greed) that measures trader emotion and sentiment. Extreme Fear (0–25) means traders are very scared; a reading of 11 suggests strong pessimism and panic selling risk.
Funding rate — A periodic payment between traders holding opposite sides of a perpetual futures contract. High funding (above 0.1% per 8 hours, or 10%+ annualized) means longs are paying shorts, signaling longs may be over-leveraged; negative funding means shorts pay longs. Funding helps keep the futures price aligned with spot.
Long/short ratio — The ratio of traders betting prices will rise (long) versus those betting prices will fall (short). A ratio of 2.19 means 2.19 longs for every short, indicating strong bullish positioning.
Mark price — The price used to calculate profits, losses, and liquidations on a perpetual futures contract. It's usually the mid-point of bid and ask prices and prevents traders from exploiting temporary price spikes to trigger liquidations.
Open interest (OI) — The total value of all open leveraged positions on a futures exchange at a given moment. High OI means many traders are using leverage; a sudden drop in OI often signals liquidations.
Open interest change — How much open interest grew or shrank during the day. Growth often signals traders are adding leverage (bullish if prices rise with it); sharp drops signal forced liquidations.
Perpetual futures or perpetual swap — A leveraged contract that trades forever without an expiration date, unlike regular futures. Traders can go long (bet on higher prices) or short (bet on lower prices) with their own money multiplied by leverage—meaning both bigger wins and bigger losses.
Taker buy/sell ratio — The ratio of market buy orders (aggressive buying) to market sell orders (aggressive selling) on a venue. Above 1.0 means more buyers are willing to buy at the ask; below 1.0 means more sellers are willing to sell at the bid, suggesting selling pressure.
Top-position ratio — The long/short ratio among the largest leveraged traders on an exchange, not all traders. It shows what the biggest money is betting on and can be a leading indicator.
Venue — A crypto exchange platform (Binance, Bybit, OKX, etc.) where futures are traded. Venues can have slightly different prices and funding rates.
Volume — The total dollar value of contracts traded on an exchange during the day. High volume usually means strong momentum and deep liquidity; low volume suggests thin order books and wider bid-ask spreads.
