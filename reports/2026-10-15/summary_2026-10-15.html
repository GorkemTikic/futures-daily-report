🗿 The day, explained simply

A big economic report on Thursday morning showed that factory prices in the United States shot up way faster than expected — 1.3% instead of 0.6%. This made people worried the Federal Reserve might raise interest rates again, and everyone got nervous. At the same time, South Korea's stock market crashed about 8% because chip companies looked too expensive and people got scared about them spending too much money on artificial intelligence. Bitcoin and Ethereum went up just a little bit, but thousands of traders who borrowed money to bet on crypto had to sell when prices dropped, and a lot of people pulled money out of Bitcoin and Ethereum investment funds. At the end of the day, a company called Ledger announced a new device to protect wallets from hackers. It was a nervous day, not calm.

07 · Glossary

Every term used today, in plain words

Annualized percentage — A percentage rate calculated as if it applied for a full year. For example, a funding rate of 0.00005429 (per 8-hour period) annualizes to ~5.94% by multiplying by the number of 8-hour periods in a year.
Basis — The type of time period used to calculate daily price changes. 'UTC-day' means the data covers midnight to midnight in Coordinated Universal Time (UTC). 'Rolling-24h' means a continuous 24-hour window.
Breadth — The percentage of all assets in a market that rose in price during the day. 50% breadth means roughly equal numbers of winners and losers.
CARF — Common Reporting Standard for Crypto Assets. An OECD framework for countries to share information about cryptocurrency account holders' identities and transactions for tax purposes, similar to the long-standing FATCA standard for bank accounts.
CBDC — Central Bank Digital Currency. A digital version of a country's official money, issued and controlled by the central bank rather than by commercial banks or private companies.
Circuit breaker — An automatic trading halt triggered when an index or market falls by a preset percentage (e.g., 8% on South Korea's KOSPI) within a short time. The halt pauses trading for 5–20 minutes to prevent panic selling.
ETF — Exchange-Traded Fund. A fund that holds Bitcoin or Ethereum and trades on a stock exchange like a regular stock. Net inflow means investors are buying the fund; net outflow means they are redeeming shares.
Funding rate — An 8-hour interest payment between long and short traders in perpetual futures. Positive rates mean longs pay shorts (bullish sentiment); negative rates mean shorts pay longs (bearish sentiment). Annualized, these small rates add up.
Hardware wallet — A physical device (like a USB stick or smart card) that stores cryptocurrency private keys offline, away from the internet. Examples: Ledger, Trezor. Supposed to be tamper-proof.
Hyperscaler — A massive technology company (e.g., Google, Amazon, Microsoft, Meta) that builds and operates huge data centers. In 2026, hyperscalers are the biggest spenders on artificial intelligence infrastructure.
Liquidation — When a trader's margin position is forcibly closed because losses exceed their collateral. In crypto, a liquidation cascade happens when many leveraged traders are forced to sell at once, often triggered by a rapid price drop.
Long/short ratio — The ratio of how many traders are betting on prices going up (long) versus down (short). A ratio of 1.77 means 1.77 long bets for every 1 short bet, indicating more bullish sentiment.
Margin — Borrowed money used to increase the size of a position. A trader with $1,000 and 10× margin can control $10,000 of Bitcoin. If Bitcoin drops 10%, the trader loses everything. Margin trading is risky.
Mark price — The reference price used to calculate profit/loss on a perpetual futures contract. It's designed to prevent manipulation by averaging recent trades and the underlying spot price.
Open interest — The total number of unfilled long and short contracts open in a market (measured in coins or in dollars). Rising open interest suggests traders are building new positions; falling suggests they are closing them.
Perpetual futures (perp) — A futures contract with no expiration date, allowing traders to bet on future price movements with leverage. Unlike standard futures, perps use a funding rate instead of a settlement date.
Point-in-time — Data captured at a single specific moment (e.g., 00:00 UTC on October 15). Funding rates and open interest are point-in-time; they change continuously. Do not compare them across different times without noting the time difference.
Real-world asset (RWA) — A physical or traditional financial asset (e.g., stocks, bonds, commodities, real estate) that is tokenized and traded on a blockchain.
Taker buy/sell ratio — A ratio of aggressive market buy orders to aggressive market sell orders. Above 1.0 means more buying pressure; below 1.0 means more selling pressure. A ratio of 0.97 means slightly more aggressive sellers.
Top-position ratio — The long-to-short ratio among the largest traders (the top 10% by account size) in a market. A higher ratio (e.g., 1.54) indicates the biggest players are betting bullish.
