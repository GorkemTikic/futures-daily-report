🗿 The day, explained simply

On September 24th, a big crypto exchange called Bitget lost about $351 million — someone hacked their computer wallets and stole it. At the same time, the US government's Federal Reserve (the bank regulator) and a trader regulator called the CFTC both released new rules for crypto companies: the Federal Reserve said stablecoin issuers (companies making dollar-like tokens) have to keep full dollar reserves and follow strict capital rules, and the CFTC said trading firms can now use crypto blockchain records officially. Also on the same day, New York's Attorney General sued a prediction market called Polymarket, saying it was running an illegal gambling operation and needs a license. Bitcoin and Ethereum barely moved (up about 0.01-0.12%), and most of the market went down 2.4%, so it was a day about rules and bad news for one exchange, not about price swings. It was a concerning day because of the hack and new scrutiny from government.

The three that mattered most

Bitget loses $351.6 million in hot wallet hack, suspends withdrawals
Hackers drained $351.6 million from Bitget's hot and warm wallets in under an hour, detected at approximately 18:31 UTC on September 24th. CEO Gracy Chen said cold wallets and user funds remained secure, and the exchange promised a $464 million user-protection fund would cover the loss. Withdrawals were suspended while the exchange investigated the breach.
Multi-chain (ETH, USDC, USDT, BNB, AVAX) · 18:31 UTC, September 24 · CoinDesk
New York Attorney General sues Polymarket, alleges illegal gambling operation
New York AG Letitia James and Governor Kathy Hochul filed suit against QCX LLC (Polymarket's US entity) on September 24th, accusing it of running an unlicensed gambling business that fails to verify user age, collect state taxes, and obtain a Gaming Commission license. The state seeks to block the platform, recover illegal gains, and impose fines triple the profits. Polymarket counter-sued later that day in federal court to block the state action.
N/A (prediction market) · September 24 · CoinDesk
Federal Reserve proposes stablecoin capital and redemption rules under GENIUS Act
The US Federal Reserve released two proposals on September 24th to regulate payment stablecoins issued by banks under the GENIUS Act. Banks must back stablecoins 1:1 with high-quality reserves (short-term Treasury bills, cash), meet capital standards, and allow redemption within two days. The proposals open a 60-day public comment period and establish the legal framework for fed-supervised banks to issue stablecoins.

Regulation and policy

CFTC clarifies tokenized assets and blockchain recordkeeping are now official tools for US commodity firms
The US Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) released updated guidance on September 24th welcoming tokenized assets and blockchain-based records as legitimate elements of regulated trading. The move gives commodity firms and brokerages explicit permission to use crypto infrastructure, following the failed CLARITY Act vote in Congress.

07 · Glossary

Every term used today, in plain words

Basis — The difference between the current price of an asset and its price at a fixed point in the past (e.g., daily basis means the change from market open to close within a single UTC calendar day).
Bitcoin (BTC) — The largest and oldest cryptocurrency by market value, created in 2009. Serves as digital gold and a store of value for many investors and institutions.
Cold wallet — A cryptocurrency storage system that is offline and disconnected from the internet, making it extremely difficult for hackers to access. Used by exchanges to secure most user funds.
Dominance — The percentage of the total cryptocurrency market value held by a single asset (e.g., Bitcoin dominance of 58.6% means Bitcoin is worth 58.6% of all cryptocurrencies combined).
Ethereum (ETH) — The second-largest cryptocurrency by market value. A blockchain platform that lets developers build applications and smart contracts on top of it.
Exchange — A platform where traders can buy and sell cryptocurrencies. Examples include Binance, Bybit, OKX, Bitget, and Gate—all major venues covered in this report.
Fear and Greed Index — A daily sentiment gauge (0–100 scale) that measures whether traders are fearful (low numbers) or greedy/confident (high numbers). 71 = Greed.
Funding rate — A small fee that long traders (bet-on-up) pay short traders (bet-on-down) every 8 hours on futures contracts. High positive rates mean longs are overconfident and paying; negative rates mean shorts are paying. Annualized % means the fee if it stayed the same for one year.
Futures contract — A bet on the future price of an asset (Bitcoin, Ethereum, stock indices, etc.) that is settled in cash. Traders can go long (bet price up) or short (bet price down), often using leverage (borrowed money), which amplifies both gains and losses.
Gold — A precious metal traditionally held as a store of value and hedge against inflation. On the report, tracked as a spot price (ounces vs. USD) and via commodity contracts.
Hot wallet — A cryptocurrency storage system that is connected to the internet for fast access to funds. More convenient than cold storage but more vulnerable to hacking.
Leverage — Borrowed money that a trader uses to control a larger bet than their own capital would allow. E.g., 10x leverage = control $1,000 worth of an asset with only $100 of your own money. Magnifies both gains and losses.
Long / Long position — A bet that an asset's price will go up. A trader who is long holds the asset or a futures contract betting on upward movement.
Long/short ratio — The number of open long bets divided by the number of open short bets. E.g., 1.16:1 = 16% more traders are betting up than down. A ratio above 1 signals bullish sentiment; below 1 signals bearish sentiment.
Mark price — The current fair value price of a futures contract on an exchange, used to settle profits/losses. It reflects both the spot price (real-world price) and funding rates to prevent extreme divergence.
Mover / Price mover — An asset that has moved sharply in price (up or down) over a given period, usually attracting trader attention.
Nasdaq Composite — An index of all stocks listed on the Nasdaq stock exchange, weighted by market value. Serves as a gauge of US technology company health.
NAV / Net asset value — The total value of assets a fund or company holds, minus liabilities. For a Bitcoin treasury company, NAV = total Bitcoin held at current price minus debt. When a company's stock trades below NAV, it means traders value the company less than the cash value of its holdings.
NYSE / US equities — The New York Stock Exchange and US stock market. The report tracks major US stock indices (S&P 500, Nasdaq) to show how US equities move relative to crypto.
Open interest (OI) — The total amount of money borrowed and deployed in open futures contracts at an exchange. Rising OI = traders adding fresh bets (bullish or bearish). Falling OI = traders closing bets (less conviction). Reported in both coins (e.g., BTC) and USD equivalent.
Open-interest change — The increase or decrease in total open interest over a single day. A positive change means new leverage entered the market; a negative change means leverage was unwound.
Perpetual / Perp — A futures contract with no expiration date (unlike traditional futures that expire). Traders can hold it as long as they like, paying funding fees every 8 hours.
S&P 500 (SPY) — The US stock market index of the 500 largest publicly traded US companies, weighted by market value. Broad gauge of US equity health. SPY is an ETF that tracks it.
Settlement — The final delivery or cash transfer when a contract or transaction ends. On exchanges, futures are usually settled in cash at the contract price.
Short / Short position — A bet that an asset's price will go down. A trader who is short borrows the asset or takes a derivatives contract betting on a decline.
Stablecoin — A cryptocurrency designed to hold a stable value, usually pegged 1:1 to the US dollar or another currency. Examples: USDT (Tether), USDC (Circle), SoFiUSD. Used for payments and as a safe harbor during market volatility.
Tokenized asset / Tokenized stock — A real-world asset (like shares of a company or a commodity) converted into a digital token that can be bought and sold on a blockchain. Aims to make asset ownership faster and more accessible.
Total market cap — The sum of all cryptocurrency market values, calculated by multiplying each coin's price by the number in circulation and adding them up. On Sept 24, all crypto was worth $2.89 trillion combined.
Treasury / US Treasury — US government debt securities (bonds) issued to pay for government spending. A 10-year Treasury is a 10-year bond. Rising Treasury yields mean bond prices are falling and the market expects rates or inflation to stay elevated.
Venue / Exchange venue — A specific crypto trading platform where traders place orders (e.g., Binance, Bybit, OKX).
Volume / Trading volume — The total amount of an asset bought and sold during a time period, measured in USD value (e.g., $10 billion in BTC volume in one day). High volume signals strong interest and price conviction; low volume can signal weak price support.
